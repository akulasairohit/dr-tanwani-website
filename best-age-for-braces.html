Best Age for Braces: When Should Your Child See an Orthodontist?

Written by Dr. Hemlata Tanwani, BDS, MDS
Specialist in Orthodontics & Dentofacial Orthopaedics · 15+ Years Clinical Practice

Orthodontic evaluation milestone: Why age 7 is the golden standard for children.

Most parents bring their child to an orthodontist when the crowding becomes obvious — usually around age twelve or thirteen, once all the permanent teeth have come in and the problem shows up in every photograph.

By then, some of the most useful treatment options have already closed.

The recommendation from orthodontic specialists worldwide is a first evaluation at age seven. Not braces at seven — an evaluation. That distinction is the source of most of the confusion parents have about this.

Why age seven?

Seven is not an arbitrary number. It is the point at which a child's mouth first reveals enough to be read properly.

By this age the first permanent molars have usually erupted, establishing the back-tooth relationship that determines how the bite will develop. The permanent front incisors are coming through, making crowding and protrusion visible. And critically, the jaws are still growing — which means their growth can still be guided.

The Core Principle: Guiding Jaws vs Moving Bones Later

That last point is the whole argument. A narrow upper jaw or a lower jaw positioned too far back is far easier to influence while the bones are still developing than after growth completes in the mid-teens. The correction that takes a simple appliance at eight can require extractions at fourteen, or jaw surgery at twenty.

Most children need nothing at this visit

This is worth saying plainly, because parents often avoid the appointment expecting to be sold treatment.

The large majority of children evaluated at seven need no treatment at all. They are placed on periodic observation — a short review every six to twelve months to track growth and eruption — and the family is told when, or whether, to expect treatment later.

That observation has real value: it means the right window is not missed, and treatment when it comes is planned rather than reactive.

Signs that warrant an evaluation

Some situations warrant a look sooner, or make the age-seven visit more urgent. If you observe any of the following with your child, schedule an evaluation with an experienced child orthodontist in Wakad:

Early or Very Late Loss of Baby Teeth

Baby teeth hold space for permanent ones. Lost too early, that space closes and the permanent tooth has nowhere to go.

Difficulty Chewing or Biting

A child who avoids certain foods or chews only on one side may have a bite that does not meet properly.

Mouth Breathing or Persistent Snoring

Often linked to a narrow upper jaw or airway obstruction, which affects facial development, not just sleep quality.

Thumb Sucking Past Age 4 or 5

Sustained pressure reshapes the developing palate and pushes the front teeth forward into an open bite.

Jaw Shifting Sideways on Closing

This usually indicates a crossbite, which will not correct itself and causes facial asymmetry if left unaddressed.

Crowded or Blocked-Out Front Teeth

Visible at seven or eight, and a reliable predictor of what is coming as larger adult canines and premolars arrive.

If you have noticed any of these, an evaluation is worth booking regardless of your child's age.

Phase One and Phase Two treatment

When early treatment is recommended, it usually follows a two-stage structure, which parents find confusing at first. Here is how the two phases differ:

Ages 7 to 10

Phase One (Interceptive)

Happens while baby and permanent teeth are both present. It addresses skeletal and space problems rather than pure tooth alignment.

  • Expands a narrow upper jaw with palatal expanders
  • Corrects crossbites and severe underbites
  • Creates room for crowded permanent teeth
  • Stops thumb-sucking habits deforming the palate
  • Duration: Typically 6 to 12 months using gentle appliances
Early Teens (11 to 14)

Phase Two (Comprehensive)

After a resting period during which the remaining permanent teeth erupt, Phase Two begins in the early teens.

  • Utilizes conventional braces (metal/ceramic) or aligners
  • Precise tooth-by-tooth alignment and aesthetic smile design
  • Finishes the bite for long-term jaw stability
  • Much shorter and simpler because jaw bases were aligned in Phase One

Not every child needs both phases — many need only Phase Two. The point of early evaluation is to determine which category your child falls into. Phase One is recommended only when it genuinely prevents a harder problem later.

What waiting can cost

Delaying is not neutral. Three consequences come up repeatedly when parents wait until all teeth have erupted at 14 or 15:

The Cost of Delaying:
  1. Extractions that could have been avoided: A jaw expanded at eight often accommodates all permanent teeth naturally. The same jaw at fifteen has hardened, often requiring the extraction of healthy premolars simply to create room.
  2. Impacted canines: Upper canines erupt late, around age twelve. If crowding blocks their path, they become trapped inside the bone, requiring complex surgical exposure. Detected early, space can be preserved so they erupt normally into the mouth.
  3. Surgery instead of growth guidance: Significant jaw discrepancies (such as severe underbites or recessive chins) can be modified while a child is growing. Once jaw growth completes in the mid-to-late teens, the same discrepancy may require corrective orthognathic surgery.

What happens at the first visit

For a seven-year-old the appointment is straightforward and painless — worth telling your child in advance, because anxiety about the unknown is usually the bigger obstacle.

The orthodontist examines the teeth, jaws and bite, and observes how the jaws move and meet. An in-house OPG X-ray (panoramic scan) is usually taken to show the permanent teeth still developing inside the bone, their position, and their eruption path. This is often the most informative part of the visit, because it reveals what is coming long before it arrives.

You will then be told one of three things:

Outcome 1
Normal Development

Teeth and jaws are on track. No follow-up needed for 12 months.

Outcome 2
Monitoring Growth

A minor issue is noted; we set up periodic reviews every 6-9 months.

Outcome 3
Early Intervention

A specific skeletal or habit issue exists where early action saves years of complex treatment.

No treatment starts at a first evaluation — you leave with complete clarity, clinical information, and all the time you need to think.
